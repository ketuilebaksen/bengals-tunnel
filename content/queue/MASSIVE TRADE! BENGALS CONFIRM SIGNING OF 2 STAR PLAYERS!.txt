Alright. Story number one. The Bengals have officially waived defensive tackle McKinley Jackson.

And the detail that makes this more than a routine cut comes from NFL Network's Ian Rapoport, who reported that Jackson was actually drawing trade interest before Cincinnati let him go. Sit with that for a second. Teams around the league were calling. There was a market, at least in theory. And the Bengals still ended up waiving him for nothing.

I already know the question half of you are yelling at the screen: if teams were interested, how did we not get SOMETHING back? A seventh-rounder? A conditional pick? Anything? It's a fair question. But here's the cold reality of how this league works: trade interest is not the same as a trade offer worth accepting. Every front office in football knew cut-down decisions were coming. If a team believed Jackson might hit the wire anyway, why would they hand Cincinnati an asset for a player they could potentially claim for free? Other GMs can read a depth chart too. Sometimes the market calls your bluff, and there's nothing you can do about it.

Still, this one stings a little, because we're talking about a third-round pick. That's real investment. Jackson was entering his third season in Cincinnati, and the honest truth is that it just never fully clicked. And it's not like there was nothing there — his rookie year, you could squint and see the vision. The size inside. A handful of plays where he flashed and you told yourself, okay, give him development time, give him reps, maybe he grows into a real piece of this rotation. That rookie season ended up being his best: 13 games, 15 tackles, one sack. But the consistency never showed up, and then the injuries started stacking. He battled multiple injuries as a rookie, and this summer — the single most important training camp of his career, with his roster spot on the line — he was banged up again. That is the worst possible timing a young player can have.

And here's the bigger takeaway, and Tunnel family, this connects straight back to what we talked about in yesterday's video. Remember what Joe Burrow said — that Cincinnati now has nine or ten defensive linemen they'd comfortably put on the field? THIS is what that depth looks like from the other side. It's not a fun stat anymore. It's a numbers game with real casualties. The Bengals didn't evaluate McKinley Jackson based on where he was drafted or what he might someday become. They looked at that loaded room and asked one question: who has earned it right now? And think about who Jackson was fighting for snaps against — Dexter Lawrence, the man they traded the tenth overall pick for. Jonathan Allen, a ten-year veteran. Kris Jenkins Jr., a young player on the rise. One of those guys was literally just handed a captain's patch. Jackson was climbing an uphill battle against a wall of talent, and the numbers finally caught him.

That doesn't mean his NFL story is over. Honestly, I'd be a little surprised if someone doesn't give him a shot. He's still young, he has NFL snaps behind him, and there's a reason he went in the third round. Sometimes a player just needs a different building. But for Cincinnati, the lesson is written in bold: a draft pick buys you an opportunity, never a guarantee. When a room gets deep, this league gets ruthless.

But here's the fascinating part. While the Bengals feel comfortable enough on the defensive line to cut a former third-rounder loose, there is another position where they are very clearly NOT done searching. And this second story is the one that makes me nervous — because we're talking about the men who might one day be responsible for protecting Joe Burrow.

The Bengals have added two more offensive tackles to the fight for the swing tackle job behind Orlando Brown Jr. and Amarius Mims. First, Cincinnati claimed former Eagles tackle Myles Hinton off waivers. One day later, they signed former Colts tackle Blake Freeland to the practice squad. Two new bodies, back to back, thrown into an already crowded battle. And Zac Taylor didn't hide the intent: one addition to the 53, one to the practice squad, everybody gets a chance to compete while the Bengals keep developing the depth of that line.

So the current picture: Hinton joins Jalen Rivers and Javon Foster as the swing options on the active roster, with Freeland waiting on the practice squad. And now let me give you the stat that should get everybody's attention. Blake Freeland has more NFL game experience than Rivers, Foster, and Hinton COMBINED. And he is the only one of the four who has ever played offensive tackle in a regular-season NFL game.

Read that again. The three tackles on the actual roster have zero regular-season snaps at tackle between them. That's where my anxiety kicks in. We know the plan when everyone's healthy — Brown on one side, Mims on the other. But this is the NFL. One awkward landing, one rolled-up pile, and suddenly one of these unproven names is standing between an elite edge rusher and Joe Burrow's ribcage. And after everything we covered yesterday about Burrow's injury history and how carefully he's managing his body — you understand why this position matters way more than the depth chart suggests.

So let's meet the candidates. Blake Freeland was a fourth-round pick by Indianapolis in 2023, and his rookie year gave him real, meaningful experience: 16 games, nine starts, 568 snaps at right tackle and another 131 at left tackle. In 2024 he appeared in nine more games, though mostly on special teams — just 22 offensive snaps at right tackle and one at left. Then came the brutal part: a season-ending broken leg in a 2025 preseason game. He fought all the way back, returned to Colts camp this summer, and competed for their swing job — but the preseason tape raised real questions. Per Pro Football Focus, Freeland allowed seven pressures across 102 pass-blocking snaps, tied for seventh-most in the league. So yes, he's the experience guy — but he's not a finished answer, and I think he knows it. He called the offensive line "a game of reps," believes that experience is his edge, and openly said a change of scenery could be exactly what he needs. And there's a genuinely human layer here too: his wife is eight months pregnant with their first child, staying back home in Indianapolis while he starts over in Cincinnati — with the baby due, mercifully, during the Bengals' bye week. We talk about these moves like chess pieces, but that's a man restarting his career in a new city while his family's biggest moment approaches. Respect.

Then there's Myles Hinton — maybe the most intriguing name in this whole fight. Get this: Hinton played AGAINST the Bengals on Friday, trying to win Philadelphia's swing tackle job. Days later, the Eagles cut him, Cincinnati claimed him, and he walked into the Bengals' building calling the whole thing a whirlwind. And honestly, cracking that Eagles tackle room was nearly impossible — they've got Lane Johnson, a six-time Pro Bowler and two-time All-Pro, and Jordan Mailata locked in as starters, with veteran depth stacked behind them. Hinton even admitted he might have been "a little delusional," but he always believed he was right there in that competition. I love that answer. That's the mentality you want from a guy fighting for his career.

His background is even better. Myles is the son of Chris Hinton, the seven-time Pro Bowl offensive lineman. Philadelphia drafted Myles in the sixth round, but his rookie season vanished before it started — a back injury sent him to injured reserve last August and wiped out the entire year. This preseason he logged 76 snaps for the Eagles, all at left tackle. And now he lands in a locker room full of familiar faces: Hinton played his college ball at Michigan, joining a genuine Wolverines colony in Cincinnati — D.J. Turner II, Erick All Jr., Kris Jenkins Jr., William Wagner. But the best connection involves his dad. Chris Hinton once swapped helmets after a Pro Bowl with Bengals legend, Hall of Famer, and Ring of Honor member Anthony Muñoz — and Muñoz confirmed it, even if he can't remember exactly which year. Come on. Decades later, Chris Hinton's son is trying to win the job protecting Cincinnati's quarterback. Football writes better scripts than Hollywood.

But — and this is the reality check — helmet swaps and Michigan reunions don't block edge rushers. The Bengals still have not decided who their swing tackle is. It could be Rivers, who played 16 games with seven starts as a rookie last year — but all seven of those starts came at guard. It could be Foster, whose four appearances in 2024 were all special teams, twelve snaps total. It could be Hinton, who has never played a regular-season NFL down. Freeland could climb off the practice squad and take it. And Zac Taylor is in no rush to name anyone — he said the guys have shown reasons they could earn the role, but he's not ready to declare how it unfolds, and he could even let them compete every single week all season long.

I've got mixed feelings about that. Competition? Love it. Nobody should own a job because of draft slot or reputation — make them earn it. But when we're talking about the emergency plan behind Orlando Brown Jr. and Amarius Mims, I also want STABILITY. If something happens to a starting tackle, I don't want the answer to be "the last man standing from a competition that never produced a winner." I want a guy this staff actually trusts. Because we have watched enough Bengals football to know exactly what one bad protection plan costs this franchise.

On the defensive line, Cincinnati is so deep that a former third-round pick with reported trade interest got waived — the room Burrow bragged about is eating its own. On the offensive line, it's the mirror opposite: still claiming, still signing, still auditioning, because nobody has slammed the door shut. And credit where it's due — I'd rather have a front office that keeps searching than one that pretends everything's fine. But eventually, someone has to WIN this job. The expectations around this team are too high for a question mark to be standing one snap away from Joe Burrow.
