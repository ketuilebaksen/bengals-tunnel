Alright. Story number one. The Bengals have officially named their seven captains for the season, and I think this list is a window into how this locker room sees itself right now.

On offense, the three captains are Joe Burrow, Ja'Marr Chase, and Ted Karras. And honestly? Nobody in Who Dey Nation is arguing with a single one of those. Burrow is automatic — this is his seventh straight year wearing the C, which tells you everything about how this franchise views him. He's the quarterback, the face of the building, the voice everyone turns to when the game tightens up. Ja'Marr Chase is entering his second season as a captain, and I love what that represents, because Ja'Marr stopped being "just" the superstar receiver a while ago. He's become one of the emotional engines of this offense — the confidence, the edge, the energy when the sideline needs a spark. And Ted Karras is back for his fifth season as a Bengals captain. Zero surprise. Tough, vocal, respected, dependable — he's lived through the playoff runs, the ugly stretches, the injuries, all of it. For an offensive line that gets dissected in this city like nowhere else, having Karras as the voice of that room matters. Three captains, three different heartbeats of the offense. They nailed it.

The defense is where it gets interesting. Your three defensive captains: Dexter Lawrence, Bryan Cook, and D.J. Turner II.

Dexter Lawrence might be the easiest call of the entire group, even though this is his first season in stripes. Remember how aggressively Cincinnati went and got him — trading the tenth overall pick to bring him in. That is NOT a move this franchise traditionally makes, and the message was never subtle: he wasn't brought here to be another defensive tackle. He was brought here to change the personality of that defensive front. And by every account, it's already happening. Joe Burrow himself has talked about how vocal Lawrence is, how much urgency he's injected into that defense. When your franchise quarterback is publicly noticing the presence of a defensive player who just walked in the door? That's a captain.

Bryan Cook is the more fascinating one. Like Lawrence, he's in his first season with the Bengals, and unlike Lawrence, he didn't arrive with a long captaincy résumé from his four years in Kansas City. So I completely understand the fans looking at this pick and wondering if it came too fast. But think about what Cook actually carries with him: championship experience. He knows what a winning standard looks like from the inside — the habits, the accountability, the way a building feels in January. Burrow specifically described him as one of those guys who has "been there and done it." And for a team that has spent recent seasons watching other franchises play meaningful football while Cincinnati sat home frustrated, importing someone who has lived championship habits is not a small thing. That's exactly the voice you want in a young secondary.

Which flows perfectly into story number two. Because when you hear what Joe Burrow is saying about this team right now, you understand why leadership and mentality have taken over the conversation.

Joe Burrow used one word to describe the feeling around this Bengals team. Inevitable.

And if you're a Bengals fan, you cannot hear that word come out of that man's mouth and just scroll past it. Because Burrow compared this feeling to something he experienced once before — LSU, going into 2019. And we ALL know how that story ends. Think back to where he was: transferred in from Ohio State, one season as the starter under his belt, outside world seeing a good-not-great team. But inside that building, Burrow felt something bigger. Justin Jefferson had emerged as a legit number one. Ja'Marr Chase was there too — and Burrow has admitted he didn't even fully grasp yet how special Ja'Marr would become. Seven starters back on offense, eight on defense. He knew how practices felt. He knew the offense fit him. He said it just felt... inevitable.

And then LSU detonated on college football. Burrow broke FBS records for touchdown passes and passer rating and won the Heisman. Jefferson and Chase combined for 195 catches, 3,320 receiving yards, and 38 touchdowns. Clyde Edwards-Helaire ran for 1,414 yards and 16 scores. LSU went 15-0, hung over 100 combined points on their two playoff opponents, and won the national championship. THAT is the season Joe Burrow reaches for when he describes this feeling. And seven years later, he says he senses something similar around the Cincinnati Bengals.

Guys — that is not nothing. Burrow doesn't manufacture confidence for attention. This is a man who's been to a Super Bowl, been to an AFC Championship, gotten close enough to touch it and watched it slip. When HE starts talking like this, I listen.

And the engine of his confidence? The defense. Burrow says the vibe over there is real, and he pointed straight at the defensive line depth. A year ago he privately saw that depth as a weakness. Now he believes Cincinnati has nine or ten defensive linemen they'd comfortably roll out in five-down packages. Dexter Lawrence is a huge piece of that. Jonathan Allen too. Boye Mafe is another major addition. And per Burrow, Lawrence and Allen have been vocal leaders from day one.

Why does that matter so much to the offense? Because Bengals fans know the exhaustion he's describing. How many times have we watched Burrow walk out there and felt — not hoped, FELT — that they had to score on this drive? Punt twice and you're down ten. Settle for a field goal and it might not be enough. That's do-or-die football on every possession, and it grinds a quarterback into dust. Burrow said the last couple of years felt exactly like that — and that this year, for the first time in a while, it doesn't. This team can commit to the run. It can lean on play-action. It can shape-shift by opponent. And yes, if they need him to drop back sixty times, he can still do that — he just knows it's not a recipe you survive on every week.

There's a real evolution happening here, and offensive coordinator Dan Pitcher put his finger on it. Last season, playing through the turf toe, Burrow couldn't move like himself, so he had to learn to win differently. Pitcher pointed to one snap against Miami in Week 16: Cincinnati called a downfield shot, Burrow instantly read that the Dolphins had it covered, and instead of forcing it or playing hero, he checked it down to Chase Brown — who ripped off 23 yards. Touchdown on the very next play. And the detail Pitcher loved most? Burrow never got touched.

That's the part that could change everything, because we can't talk about Joe Burrow without talking about his body. Torn ACL as a rookie. Back to carry Cincinnati to the Super Bowl in 2021. The AFC Championship the next year — the 23-20 heartbreak against Mahomes and the Chiefs team that went on to win it all. Then 2023, seven games lost to the wrist. In 2024 he played at an MVP level and the defense wasted it. Last year, nine games gone to turf toe. Three of his six seasons with significant missed time. So when Burrow talks about obsessing over nutrition, about preparing his body differently, about throwing the ball into the dirt when a free rusher comes instead of trying to make him miss — that's not him getting soft. That's him getting smart. Save the Superman cape for the fourth quarter, when the game actually demands it. Live for the next down.

And if you think the calculated version of Burrow is somehow the lesser version — look at how last season ended.
